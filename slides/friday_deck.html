SECTION ONE

The ideas, slowly

Yesterday concerned one variable. Today concerns two, which adds several ways to reach a wrong conclusion.

Start here

Today's question.

You have two measurements of the world

Global temperature, going back to 1880. Ocean temperature at Scripps Pier, going back to 1916.

The obvious question: do they move together?

And the obvious follow-up

If they do, does that tell you anything about why?

Spoiler for the whole afternoon

They move together strongly. And when we look a bit harder, most of that agreement turns out to come from the fact that both of them went up over a hundred years, which is a much weaker statement than it first appears.

Learning to spot that is the point of Friday.

Vocabulary

The terms we will use.

correlation
A single number from −1 to +1 saying how well two things rise and fall together in a straight-line way.
fitted line
The one straight line that comes closest to all your points at once.
slope
How much the second thing changes when the first goes up by one unit.
intercept
What the line predicts when the first thing is zero.
residual
For one point, how far the real value sat above or below the line.
trend
A long, slow drift in one direction over time.
first difference
This year's value minus last year's. A way of asking about changes rather than levels.

The two that get confused

Correlation has no units and doesn't tell you how big the effect is. Slope has units and does. You need both, and reporting only the first is very common.

Correlation · what it measures

Do they go up and down together?

The idea, without symbols

For each year, ask two questions. Was global temperature above its own average? Was the pier above its own average?

If the answer is usually the same for both, you have a positive correlation. If it's usually opposite, negative. If there's no pattern, near zero.

The scale

+1 is a perfect straight line going up. −1 is a perfect straight line going down. 0 is no straight-line pattern at all.

xi − x̄ For one year: how far global temperature was from its own average. Positive if that year was hot.
yi − ȳ Same year: how far the pier was from its own average.
( )( ) Multiply the two together. Both above average gives a positive. Both below also gives positive. One of each gives negative.
Σ ÷ (n−1) Add that up across every year and divide. Mostly positives, and you get a positive total. This is called the covariance.
÷ sxsy Divide by both spreads. That strips the units off and squashes the answer into the range −1 to +1.
r = sxy / (sx sy) Pearson correlation

Why bother with that last division

Without it you'd have covariance, which comes out in °C² and changes the moment somebody switches to Fahrenheit. You couldn't compare it to anything.

Dividing fixes that, at a cost: r now has no units, so it carries no information about the size of the effect. The next slide takes that up.

A unit conversion

What happens if you switch to Fahrenheit?

The slope changes

Multiplied by 9/5, and its units become °F per °C. The intercept shifts too, because of the +32.

The correlation doesn't change at all

Same number, before and after. r is unaffected by any change of units.

Why this matters

It's a useful property: you can compare correlations across variables measured in totally different things.

It's also a warning. A correlation of 0.9 sounds impressive and tells you nothing about whether the effect is big enough to care about. For that you need the slope, with its units.

Before reporting a correlation

Why a correlation needs a plot beside it.

Four datasets, similar correlations

Only the first is actually a straight-line relationship.

The second is a curve, and a straight line describes it badly. The third is two separate groups that probably shouldn't be in one analysis. In the fourth, a single distant point is doing nearly all the work.

The rule that follows

Plot it, then calculate it. Never the other way round. A correlation reported without a scatterplot is a number nobody can check.

And in climate data especially

Anything that rises with time correlates with anything else that does.

The trap

Take two completely unrelated things that both grew steadily since 1950. Their correlation will be high. There's nothing in the formula that knows the points are in time order.

Why it's worse here than elsewhere

In climate data, calendar time is not a neutral label. It stands in for changing forcings, changing instruments, changing sampling practice, changing circulation, and every process you left out.

So a correlation between two trending series is partly a statement about the twentieth century rather than about your two variables.

The rule for today

Plot both series against time first. Then show time inside the scatterplot, as color if nothing else. Only then work out r.

Notebook 09 works this way on purpose, putting the time plots in section 5 ahead of the correlation in section 6.

Fitting a line · the idea

The line that misses by the least.

What "best fit" means

Put a straight line through your cloud of points. For each point, measure how far above or below the line it sits. Square those distances and add them up.

The best line is the one where that total is as small as it can be. That's all "least squares" means.

Why square them

So that missing by 2 above and 2 below don't cancel out, and so that one big miss counts for more than several small ones.

xi The thing you're using to predict. For us, global temperature that year.
b₁xi Multiply it by the slope. Steeper slope means the pier responds more.
b₀ + Add the intercept, which shifts the whole line up or down. Without it the line would be forced through zero.
ŷi The line's predicted value for that year. The hat distinguishes a prediction from an observation.
yi − ŷi What really happened, minus the guess. That gap is the residual.
ŷi = b₀ + b₁xi the fitted line
ei = yi − ŷi the residual

The symbols, and which is which.

The pattern from yesterday, again

Greek letters are the truth we're assuming exists. Roman letters are what we calculated from the data we happen to have.

Mixing them up is how people end up claiming they measured something they estimated.

The slope is the part with meaning

The slope, and its units.

For our two variables

Global temperature anomaly goes across. Pier SST goes up the side. So the slope is in local °C per global °C.

It means: within these matched years, how much higher was annual Pier temperature, per 1 °C higher global anomaly?

A worked example

Suppose the line is Pier SST = 17.0 + 0.70 × anomaly. At an anomaly of 0.8 °C, the line predicts 17.56 °C.

If the pier actually measured 18.10 °C that year, the residual is 18.10 − 17.56 = 0.54 °C. That's what the line failed to predict.

Two sentences that sound the same and are not

Fine: "Within these years, Pier SST was on average 1.27 °C higher per 1 °C higher global anomaly."

Not fine: "The pier warms 1.27 °C when the world warms 1 °C."

The first describes the years in your sample. The second claims a mechanism you haven't tested and can't test with a scatterplot.

Residuals · what they're for

What residuals are for.

Why look at the misses

The line is a summary. If it's a good summary, the leftovers should look like formless scatter around zero.

If the leftovers have a pattern, the line missed something real, and the pattern tells you what.

Plot them three ways

Against the fitted values. Against the predictor. And against time, which is the one people forget and the one that matters most here.

A residual is not an error

It's what the line failed to predict for that year. It isn't automatically a mistake in the measurement.

Often the biggest residual is the most physically interesting year in the whole record.

Residuals · reading the patterns

Five shapes and what each one suggests.

What you seeWhat it may mean
Curved through the middleA straight line is the wrong shape; there's a bend in the real relationship
Spread widens like a funnelThe scatter grows as the values grow, so one line can't describe both ends
A few points far from the restInfluential years worth going and looking at individually
Long runs above, then long runs below zeroPersistence, a regime change, or a process the line leaves out
Early years behave unlike late yearsOne single relationship across the whole record may be the wrong model

The asymmetry to remember

A clean residual plot does not prove your assumptions were right. A patterned one is real evidence that the simple line is incomplete.

So it can catch you out, but it can't let you off.

The assumptions come attached

What the standard formulas take for granted.

The usual list

That a straight line is the right shape. That the misses average out to zero. That the observations are independent. That the scatter is about the same everywhere. That no single point is running the whole fit.

One that gets misread constantly

Assumptions about normal distributions are mainly about small samples. They are not a requirement that your temperatures be bell-shaped.

Pier SST doesn't need to look like a bell curve for a regression to be reasonable.

Why today barely mentions p-values

With a hundred-odd years of related annual values and a shared trend, a small p-value is nearly guaranteed and tells you very little about the physics.

So notebook 09 spends its effort on plots, units, residuals and sensitivity checks instead. That's a choice, not an oversight.

First differences

Changes instead of levels.

yt The value this year. The little t is just a label for which year.
yt−1 The value last year. One step back, so t minus 1.
− Subtract. 17.9 this year, 17.6 last year, so +0.3. The year got warmer by three tenths.
Δ Delta. Standard notation for a change in whatever follows it.
Δyt = yt − yt−1 a first difference

The idea

Instead of comparing how warm each year was, compare how much each year changed from the one before.

A long slow trend disappears when you do this, because it adds roughly the same small amount every year. What survives is the year-to-year wiggle.

What that buys you

If the correlation stays strong in changes, the two really do move together year by year.

If it collapses, most of what you saw was both series drifting upward across a century.

It isn't a magic fix

It changes the question from levels to changes, which may not be the question you wanted. It amplifies measurement noise. It throws away the first year. It must not silently jump over missing years. And a real climate response may be spread over decades, in which case year-to-year is the wrong place to look for it.

The limit of the whole exercise

Why none of this shows cause.

What pier temperature responds to

Upwelling. Winds. Currents. Heat exchange with the air. Climate modes like El Niño. Local weather. Where the sensor sat. And long-term warming, all at once.

What our predictor actually is

Global mean temperature is itself a result of the climate system, not something anybody set to a value on purpose. Nobody randomly assigned the twentieth century to a treatment group.

The CO₂ version, said carefully

CO₂ is a well-established driver of climate. That's solid physics from elsewhere.

But regressing this year's pier temperature on this year's Mauna Loa CO₂ is still not a measurement of that effect. Both series trend, the physics works through the whole climate system, responses lag, and dozens of processes are missing.

You can compute that slope in four lines of code. Being able to compute it isn't permission to interpret it.

What a correlation does support

A bounded statement about association in the sample you chose. That's a real and useful thing to be able to say. It's a smaller claim than people usually make, and it's the one you can defend.

Yesterday's lesson, one level up

Years are not independent either.

Yesterday, at the day scale

Adjacent days correlated at 0.46, so we resampled summers instead, and the honest interval came out 2.6 times wider.

Today, at the year scale

Take the residuals and check whether each one resembles the one before it. That's called the lag-one correlation.

You will compute it in notebook 09 section 7. It is not zero.

What a nonzero value means

The line left time structure behind, so the years are not independent pieces of evidence. Any uncertainty calculated as though they were will be too small.

What it does not mean is that you now need a complicated time-series model. Naming the problem honestly beats reaching for machinery you can't defend.

The shape of a defensible claim

What a small model can and can't say.

A careful result sounds like this

"Across the selected matched years, annual mean Pier surface SST and NASA global temperature anomaly had a fitted association of [slope with units]. The residual plot showed [pattern], and the relationship [did / didn't] remain similar for year-to-year changes. Shared trend, dependence, local processes, sampling and the observational design limit causal or predictive claims."

Don't say

"Global temperature causes this exact local change", on the strength of one scatterplot.

Don't say

That a pier-versus-CO₂ slope is a direct CO₂ effect.

Don't say

Any correlation, without having plotted both series against time.

Don't say

That a small p-value means a large or important physical effect.

Section 3 — the completeness rule

A year with 12 observed days shouldn't sit next to a year with 360 as though they were equally good annual averages.

So the notebook sets a minimum number of days in code. Visible, and arguable.

The habit again

You could have made that judgment in your head and never written it down. Then nobody, including you, could check it later.

Notebook 09 · section 4

Look at what the merge threw away.

Run it now — section 4

Join the two tables on year and see how many years survive. You should get about 107 matched years, roughly 1917 to 2025.

Some years disappeared. Which ones?

Years the pier didn't cover, and years that failed your completeness rule.

Why that's worth two minutes

Every dropped year is a decision you made. If the dropped ones are mostly the early, sparse, hand-transcribed years, then your "century-long relationship" covers less than a century without saying so.

Say it in the write-up

"107 matched years, 1917 to 2025, requiring at least 300 observed pier days per year" is a sentence a reader can evaluate. "I used the pier data" is not.

Notebook 09 · section 5 · before the scatterplot on purpose

Look at time first.

What you can already predict

Both go up. That on its own guarantees a positive correlation, before any physics enters the room.

Notice how different they look

The global line is smooth. The pier line jumps around a lot. One is an average over the whole planet; the other is one location. Averaging over a planet removes a great deal of noise.

Notebook 09 · section 6

The line, and what its numbers mean.

Three numbers

r = +0.65. Slope +1.27 °C local per °C global. Intercept 17.03 °C.

Why the intercept is meaningful here

An anomaly of zero means the 1951–1980 baseline, which is a real period, not an impossible extrapolation. So 17.03 °C is roughly what the line says the pier looked like then.

It's still a fitted summary, not a measurement of that period.

Look at the colors

Points are colored by year, and they march steadily from one corner to the other. That's time, sitting inside your scatterplot.

That becomes the subject three slides from here.

Notebook 09 · section 7

What the line left behind.

Read the right-hand panel

These are the misses, plotted in time order. They aren't scattered randomly. There are long stretches sitting above zero and long stretches below.

Lag-one correlation: +0.33. If a year was above the line, the next year probably was too.

That's a row from the table earlier

"Long runs above, then long runs below zero." Persistence, a regime shift, or a process the line leaves out.

It means our 107 years are not 107 independent pieces of evidence, which is yesterday's lesson arriving one level up. It doesn't make the slope wrong. It limits how tightly we can claim to know it.

Notebook 09 · section 8

How much of that was just the trend?

0.65 becomes 0.16

Same years. Same two variables. Same code. The only difference is the question.

Left: "were warm years warm in both?" Right: "when the globe warmed more than last year, did the pier?"

The answer to the second one is: barely.

What that means, carefully

Most of the correlation on the left came from both series drifting upward over a century, rather than from them moving together year by year.

This is not a debunking of anything. Long-term warming is real and it's in both records. It means this particular scatterplot mostly summarizes calendar time, and your written claim should say so.

Notebook 09 · what you're making

One bounded statement, in your own words.

Write this in the notebook before you leave

Four or five sentences, with your own numbers. It should read as measured rather than striking.

A worked version to copy the shape of

"Across 107 matched years from 1917 to 2025, requiring at least 300 observed pier days per year, annual mean Pier surface temperature and NASA global temperature anomaly had a correlation of +0.65 and a fitted slope of +1.27 °C local per °C global. The residuals showed long runs of the same sign with a lag-one correlation of +0.33, so the years are not independent and the line leaves time structure unexplained. Repeating the comparison on year-to-year changes rather than levels dropped the correlation to +0.16, which indicates that most of the levels relationship reflects shared long-term trend. Shared trend, dependence between years, local processes such as upwelling, the completeness rule I chose, and the observational design all limit any causal or predictive claim."

Why this is the deliverable

Anybody can produce r = 0.65 in one line of code. The paragraph above is the part that took judgment, and it's the part a supervisor will actually rely on.

SECTION THREE

Your own small analysis

One question, one dataset you already have, one figure, one number, and an honest paragraph. Thirty to sixty minutes of work.

The brief

Five things to hand in.

  1. A question you can answer with data you already have. Small and specific.
  2. The data, loaded from your own saved copy, with the source written down.
  3. One figure, with a title, both axes labeled, and units.
  4. One number (an average, a difference, a correlation, a slope) with its units.
  5. Four to eight sentences saying what it means, including at least one honest limitation.

Where to work

Start from notebooks/10_final_analysis.ipynb. The structure is already laid out with prompts to fill in.

Work in your own course folder. Hand in the .ipynb with the outputs saved in it.

How big is right

"Is the surface-bottom difference bigger in summer than winter?" is a good project.

"How is climate change affecting the California Current?" is a research program, not an afternoon.

You already have all of these

Pick one of these.

Scripps Pier temperature

Daily surface and bottom readings, 1916 to now, with quality flags.

Seasonal cycles, stratification, extreme events, long-term change.

Scripps Pier salinity

Same record, same structure, a different measurement. We barely touched it this week, which makes it a good choice if you want something of your own.

NASA GISTEMP

Global temperature anomaly, monthly and annual, 1880 to now.

Mauna Loa CO₂

The Keeling curve, monthly. A very clean seasonal cycle sitting on top of a steady rise.

Two questions you're welcome to steal

Has the size of the seasonal swing in pier temperature changed between the first thirty years of the record and the last thirty?

Does the seasonal swing in the Keeling curve have a trend of its own, separate from the overall rise?

Both are answerable in an afternoon, both need a real decision from you, and neither has an answer you can look up.

Checking your own work

Three tests before you hand it in.

The figure test

Could somebody who missed this week understand your figure without you standing next to it? Title, both axis labels, units, and a caption saying what it shows.

The provenance test

Could somebody get your exact input file using only your notes? Source, date you retrieved it, filename.

The restart test

Restart the kernel and run the whole notebook from top to bottom. If it fails, it was never reproducible; it only worked in the order you happened to click.

The sentence that earns the most credit

The limitation. "This is one pier, so it describes this spot and not the California coast." "Values before 1950 were hand-transcribed and I haven't checked them." "The years are not independent of each other."

Naming a real weakness in your own result is the clearest possible signal that you understood what you did.

Using AI on this

Allowed. Say so in the notebook: what you asked for, what it gave you, and what you checked.

An unverified generated figure with a plausible-looking axis label is the failure this whole week was about.

Where this leaves you

What to take with you.

Write down where it came from

A result you can't trace is a result you can't defend.

Look before you summarize

Every summary hides a shape. Plot it first.

Name your assumptions

The resampling unit, the completeness rule, the straight line. All of them were choices.

Say what it can't show

The limitation is not a weakness in the work. It's part of the work.

None of this needed you to be a programmer. It required you to be the kind of scientist who can still explain, six months from now, what you did and why.
