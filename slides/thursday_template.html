SECTION ONE

The ideas, slowly

No code in this section. Just the handful of words that everything else today depends on.

Start here

Why we cannot just report the average.

Say you measure the ocean at the pier for one summer

You take the average. You get a number. Someone asks: "if you had measured a different summer, would you have got the same number?"

Almost certainly not. So which number is the real one?

That question has a whole toolkit behind it

The honest answer is not one number. It is a number plus a range, and a sentence saying what that range assumes.

What you will be able to say by 4pm

"The average summer surface-minus-bottom difference at Scripps Pier is 1.33 °C, and if I had a different set of summers I would expect to get something between 1.25 and 1.41 °C."

That second half is today.

Three words, used carefully

These are not the same thing.

The observations

What was actually measured. The rows in your file. Every one is real, and every one is different from the others.

The statistic

A summary you calculate from those rows. One number standing in for thousands.

An average is a statistic. So is a median, or a maximum.

The interval

A range showing how much that summary would move if you could go back and collect data again.

It describes the summary, not the ocean.

If you remember one thing from this section

Most confusion in statistics comes from mixing up the second and the third. We are going to separate them very carefully.

The words we will use today

Plain English first.

population
Everything you wish you could measure. Usually impossible to measure all of it.
sample
The part you actually got.
observation
One measurement. One row.
mean
The average. Add them up, divide by how many.
median
The middle one, when you line them up in order.
standard deviation
How far a typical single measurement sits from the average.
standard error
How much the average itself would wobble if you collected data again.
bootstrap
A way of finding that wobble by reusing the data you already have.

Two of those sound identical and are not

Standard deviation and standard error. We are going to spend a whole slide on the difference, with a picture, because getting them mixed up is the single most common mistake in this material.

Population and sample

You never have all of it.

An everyday version

You want to know the average height of everyone at Scripps. You cannot measure everyone, so you measure the forty people in this room.

Everyone at Scripps is the population. The forty of you are the sample. Each person measured is one observation.

Our version today

We want the typical June–August surface-minus-bottom temperature difference at the pier, across all summers, including ones nobody measured.

What we have is the summers in the archive. That is our sample.

The catch, and it is a big one

The forty people in this room are not a random selection of Scripps. Neither are the summers in an archive a random selection of summers.

Rows in a file are not automatically a fair sample. How, when and why the measurements were taken stays part of your analysis. No formula today fixes a problem there.

Saying the symbols

μ is "mew", a Greek letter. It stands for the number we would get if we could measure everything. We never see it.

x̄ is "x-bar". It is the number we actually calculated. It is our best guess at μ, and it is not μ.

Describing what you have · 1 of 2

The middle of the data.

x̄ = (1/n) Σ xi

That symbol just means "add them all up"

Σ is a capital Greek S, for "sum". So the formula says: add up all the values, then divide by how many there are.

It is the average you already know. The notation is only there so it fits on one line.

The mean

Add everything, divide by the count. Sensitive to extreme values: one enormous number drags it.

The median

Line the values up smallest to largest and take the middle one. One enormous number barely moves it.

Why report both

If the mean and median are close, the data is roughly symmetric. If they are far apart, something is pulling on one side, and you want to know that before you summarize with either.

Describing what you have · 2 of 2

How spread out it is.

s² = (1/(n−1)) Σ (xi − x̄)²

s = √s²the standard deviation

What that is doing, step by step

Take each value. Subtract the average, so you get how far off it was. Square it, so that being 2 below counts the same as 2 above. Add all those up. Divide. Then square-root it to get back to the original units.

What comes out

One number saying how far a typical single measurement sits from the average. Small means the values are all bunched together. Large means they are scattered.

Why n − 1 and not n

A small technical correction. Dividing by n − 1 stops the answer being slightly too small on average.

You do not need to derive it. You do need to know that this correction assumes your measurements are independent of each other, which ours are not. We come back to that.

Do this every single time

Check the units.

In °C

The mean. The median. The quantiles. The IQR. The standard deviation. All of these come out in the same units as your data.

In °C²

The variance, because we squared things and did not square-root them back. This is why we usually report s rather than s².

No units at all

A count. A correlation. A proportion.

Why this is a real check and not busywork

If you calculate something and the units come out impossible, you have not made a rounding error. You have made a mistake in the calculation or in what you think you are calculating.

It costs five seconds and it catches things nothing else catches.

One tiny example, by hand

Four numbers, all the way through.

1, 2, 3, 4 °Cthe entire dataset

x̄ = 2.5 °C

Σ(xi − x̄)² = 5 °C²

s = √(5 / 3) ≈ 1.29 °C

Follow the arithmetic

Average of 1, 2, 3, 4 is 2.5. The distances from 2.5 are −1.5, −0.5, +0.5, +1.5. Squared: 2.25, 0.25, 0.25, 2.25. That adds to 5.

Divide by n − 1, which is 3, giving 1.67. Square root: about 1.29 °C.

What the two numbers say

The middle of this data is 2.5 °C. A typical value sits about 1.3 °C away from that middle.

What they do not say

Whether the data is lopsided. Whether it has two separate humps. Whether there are gaps. What order it came in.

Two datasets can have the same mean and the same standard deviation and look nothing like each other. So we always plot it too.

Now the real thing

A century of summer days at the pier.

How to read this

Each bar counts how many days had that surface-minus-bottom difference. Tall bar near 0 means lots of days where surface and bottom were about the same.

Two things to notice

It is lopsided. There is a long tail stretching right, so the mean sits above the median. Reporting only "1.32 °C" hides that completely.

And hold on to 1.49 °C, the standard deviation. It is about to be confused with a very different number.

Changing the question

Stop. Different question now.

Everything so far answered this

"How much do individual days vary?"

Answer: about 1.49 °C either side of the average. That is the standard deviation.

Here is a completely different question

"If I went and collected a whole new hundred years of summer days, how different would my average come out?"

That is not about days. It is about the average itself.

Why they feel like the same question

Both are "how much does it vary". Both come out in °C. Both get called "the spread". But one is about single measurements and one is about a summary of thousands of measurements, and their answers are nowhere near each other.

Next slide has both, drawn on the same axis.

The most important picture today

Same axis. Two different questions.

Top panel: the days

Wide. Individual days really do vary by more than a degree. s = 1.49 °C.

Bottom panel: the average

A spike. Across thousands of imagined repeats, the average barely moves. 0.016 °C.

Both are true at once

Any given day is unpredictable to more than a degree. The long-run average of thousands of days is pinned down very tightly.

That is not a contradiction. It is the reason averages are useful.

Naming the second one

Standard error, and where it comes from.

SE(x̄) = s / √nhow much the average would wobble, if the observations were independent

Reading it

Take the spread of the data. Divide by the square root of how many you have. That is it.

With s = 1.49 and n = 8,771: √8,771 is about 94, and 1.49 ÷ 94 gives 0.016 °C.

Why more data helps

The bottom of the fraction grows as you collect more, so the answer shrinks. Four times as much data halves it.

The words that matter in that gloss

"If the observations were independent." That phrase is doing enormous work, and we have not checked it. Two slides from now we will, and it turns out to be false.

A useful thing to see once

More data buys certainty, not a calmer ocean.

The red line falls

The more days you collect, the more sure you become about the long-run average.

The blue line does not move

Individual days are just as variable as they always were. Measuring more of them does not calm the ocean down.

The sentence people get wrong

"We have loads of data, so the temperature does not vary much." No. Loads of data means you know the average well. Tomorrow is still unpredictable.

The assumption we skipped

Are our days independent?

What this plot is

Every dot is one day. Across the bottom: that day's difference. Up the side: the next day's difference.

What it shows

If days were independent, knowing today would tell you nothing about tomorrow, and this would be a shapeless cloud.

It is not a cloud. It slopes. r = 0.46. Warm days follow warm days.

So what?

Our 8,771 days are not 8,771 separate pieces of information. Many of them are partly repeats of the day before.

The formula that gave 0.016 °C assumed otherwise. It is arithmetically correct and it is answering a question about data we do not have.

Where dependence comes from

This is normal, not a flaw in your data.

The ocean has memory

A warm patch of water is still there tomorrow. Weather systems last days.

One event, many rows

A single storm can produce a week of unusual readings that all say the same thing.

Seasons

August days resemble other August days more than they resemble January.

Shared equipment

One instrument, one calibration, one processing pipeline behind every row.

What an honest analyst does about it

Not "fix" it. Name it, pick a unit of data that is more defensible, and report how much the answer changes if you had picked differently.

That is the whole of section 3 this afternoon, and it changes our answer by a factor of nearly three.

Ranges · 1 of 2

What a confidence interval actually claims.

x̄ ± t* · SE(x̄)the estimate, plus and minus a multiple of the standard error

The careful sentence

If you repeated the whole sampling procedure many times, and calculated an interval the same way each time, about 95% of those intervals would contain the true value.

The claim is about the method, over many imagined repeats.

An analogy that helps

Think of a ring toss. The method is your throwing arm; the peg is the true value. Saying "95%" describes how often your arm lands a ring over the peg.

Once a specific ring has landed, it either went over the peg or it did not. The 95% was about your arm, not about that one throw.

Ranges · 2 of 2

The version everyone says, which is wrong.

Try not to say

"There is a 95% probability that the true value is inside this interval."

Once you have calculated one interval, the true value is not moving around inside it. It is fixed. Your interval either covers it or it does not.

Does this matter in practice?

Rarely for you, honestly. It matters when a reviewer challenges you, and it matters because the sloppy version leads people to treat the interval as a probability distribution over the truth, which it is not.

And one more distinction

The sampling distribution is the spread of the statistic across imagined repeats. It is not the histogram of your data.

That is the two-panel picture from earlier: top was the data, bottom was the sampling distribution.

Bootstrap · the idea

When you would rather not trust a formula.

The problem

The formula s/√n needs independent observations. Ours are not. And for statistics other than the mean, there may be no tidy formula at all.

The trick

We wanted to know what would happen if we collected the data again. We cannot. So we pretend the data we have is the world, and draw new samples from it.

Picture it as a hat

Write each of your values on a slip of paper and put them in a hat. Draw one out, write it down, put it back, and draw again, until you have as many as you started with.

Some values get drawn twice, some not at all. Take the average of that new pile. Then do the whole thing a few thousand times.

The spread of those few thousand averages is your answer.

Bootstrap · the steps

Five steps, and one of them is yours alone.

  1. Decide what you are estimating, and what counts as one unit that could be swapped for another.
  2. Draw that many units out of the hat, with replacement.
  3. Calculate your statistic on the new pile.
  4. Do it a few thousand more times.
  5. Take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the results. That is your 95% interval.

Steps 2 to 5 are the computer's job

It does them instantly, thousands of times, without complaining or getting tired.

Step 1 is entirely yours

Is one unit a day? A storm? A month? A whole summer? The computer cannot tell you, will not warn you, and the answer changes depending on what you choose.

This afternoon you will see it change by a factor of 2.6.

Last one before we open a notebook

What none of this repairs.

An unfair sample

A tight interval around unrepresentative data is a very precise description of the wrong thing.

Wrong units or misread flags

A Fahrenheit column or a misunderstood quality flag gives a confident number that is wrong.

Gaps that are not random

If the sensor fails in storms, you are missing exactly the conditions you care about.

Cause

No interval turns "these two move together" into "this one causes that one".

Which is why the order today is what it is

Where the data came from, then look at it properly, then say clearly what you are estimating, and only then put a range on it.

Notebook 07 is the "look at it properly" step, and it comes first for a reason. Do it the other way round and you get a beautifully quantified mistake.

What goes in it

How many rows. What date range. How many values are missing in each column. How many rows have both temperatures. What the quality flags say. Any repeated dates. Anything physically impossible.

What you are looking for

Not perfection. A hundred-year hand-written record will have holes in it.

The question to take away from it

"Given these gaps, is my summer average a fair summary of summers? Or is it a summary of the summers when the instrument happened to be working?"

You often cannot answer that fully. Saying so in your write-up is part of the result, not an admission of failure.

Notebook 07 · section 4

Look at the shape before you summarize it.

Run it now — section 4

Compare the surface and bottom distributions, and read the mean, median, IQR and standard deviation with their units.

What to say about what you see

Is it roughly symmetric, or lopsided? Is there one hump or two? Are the mean and median close together?

Why it changes what you do next

If the shape is strongly lopsided, the mean is a slightly misleading summary and the median may be better. If there are two humps, you may really have two different situations mixed together.

Connecting it forward

The s you calculate here is the same s that goes into s/√n in the next notebook. If the shape is odd, that formula is describing something you may not have meant.

Notebook 07 · section 5 · the trap

A value sits far from the others.

What you will want to do

Delete it. The plot looks tidier afterwards, the standard deviation drops, and everything feels cleaner.

Why that is dangerous here

Upwelling, storms and marine heatwaves all produce extreme days at this pier that are entirely real. Those days are frequently the scientifically interesting ones.

Deleting values because they are inconvenient is how people accidentally delete their own finding.

The phrase to hold on to

Extreme does not automatically mean wrong.

An unusual measurement and a broken measurement look identical in a spreadsheet. Telling them apart takes knowing something about the ocean, not about statistics.

Notebook 07 · section 5 · what to do instead

Impossible, or just unusual?

Impossible → investigate and probably remove

−40 °C at Scripps Pier. A surface reading of 999. A date in the year 1815.

These are instruments failing, or fill values that were never meant to be read as data.

Unusual → keep, and look at it

A 7 °C surface-minus-bottom difference in August. A single very cold week in July.

These are the ocean doing something. Go and find out what.

The defensible procedure

Remove only what you can justify by a physical reason. Write down what you removed and why. If removing it changes your answer, report the answer both ways.

"I dropped three days where the sensor reported −999, which is the archive's missing-data marker" is defensible. "I dropped the outliers" is not.

Run it now, then answer the exit ticket

Run section 5, look at the days around each extreme value, and for each one ask: broken instrument, or real ocean?

Notebook 07 · what you are making

A one-page data health statement.

Six things it contains

Where the data came from and when you got it. The window and quality rule you chose. What is missing, and whether the gaps look random. What you removed and why. One sentence on what this record can support. One sentence on what it cannot.

Why this is worth the twenty minutes

Every number you produce for the rest of the week rests on it. Writing it once means you never have to reconstruct from memory what "the good data" meant.

It is also, almost word for word, the paragraph a reviewer will ask you for, and the briefing an AI needs so it stops guessing about your data.

SECTION THREE

Putting a range on it

The data is inspected, so now we can estimate something honestly. The interesting part is not the arithmetic. It is one choice that changes the answer by a factor of nearly three.

Notebook 08 · sections 1 and 2

Calculate the easy answer first, so we can distrust it.

Everyone — open notebook 08 now

  1. Same folder. Open notebooks/08_uncertainty.ipynb, kernel climate-data-foundations.
  2. Run section 1. It rebuilds the checked dataset using yesterday's rules.
  3. Run section 2 and read the two numbers it prints.

What you should see

About 8,771 summer days. A spread of about 1.49 °C. And a standard error of about 0.016 °C.

Taken at face value, that last number says we know the average summer difference to within a few hundredths of a degree.

Believe it for thirty seconds

You have already seen the lag plot. Adjacent days correlate at 0.46. The formula that produced 0.016 assumed they did not.

So this is not bad arithmetic. It is correct arithmetic answering a question about a dataset we do not have, which is a much harder mistake to spot.

Notebook 08 · section 3

Turn 8,771 days into 100 summers.

Run it now — section 3

This collapses each summer down to a single average, leaving about 100 numbers instead of 8,771.

Why throw away so much

Because those 8,771 rows were never 8,771 independent pieces of information. Days within one summer are heavily related to each other.

Two different summers, though, are much closer to being separate. A hundred summers is a hundred fairly independent observations of "what a summer is like".

What we keep

Each summer's average still contains all of that summer's days. We have not deleted anything. We have grouped it into units that we can defend treating as exchangeable.

It should feel wasteful

It does, and it is still right. Having 8,771 rows never meant having 8,771 rows' worth of information.

Notebook 08 · section 4

Now do the hat trick, four thousand times.

What this histogram is, and is not

It is not the data. It is not the summers. It is 4,000 answers to the question "what if we had happened to get a different set of summers?"

The width of that pile is the uncertainty.

Reading the interval

Cut off the lowest 2.5% and the highest 2.5% of those 4,000 answers. What is left runs from 1.25 to 1.41 °C.

That is the 95% bootstrap interval, and no formula was involved.

Notebook 08 · section 5 · the point of the whole day

One choice. A 2.6× difference.

Same data. Same statistic. Same code.

The only thing that changed is what counted as one unit in the hat.

Days: the average is pinned to about ±0.03 °C. Summers: about ±0.08 °C.

Which one is right?

The summers, here, because we can see on the lag plot that days are related to each other.

But notice the honest version is not "always pick the wider one". It is that the interval describes your model, not the ocean. You have to be able to defend the model, and you have to report which one you picked.

Notebook 08 · what you are making

Three sentences. Write them before you leave.

1 · The estimate

What you estimated, the value, and the units.

2 · The interval, and the unit you resampled

The range, how you got it, and what counted as one unit. This is the sentence people leave out.

3 · What it does not cover

Sampling, instruments, gaps, and the fact that this is one record from one pier.

A worked version, to copy the shape of

"The mean summer surface-minus-bottom difference at Scripps Pier is 1.33 °C, with a 95% bootstrap interval of 1.25 to 1.41 °C, resampling whole summers as the unit. Resampling individual days instead would give an interval 2.6 times narrower, which the day-to-day correlation of 0.46 tells us would be overconfident. This interval says nothing about sensor calibration, about gaps in the record, or about whether these summers represent summers we did not observe."

Where this leaves you

The number was the easy part.

Look first

A range around unexamined data is precision about the wrong thing.

Name the unit

The computer resamples whatever you tell it to. That choice is the analysis.

Report the model

An interval describes your assumptions as much as it describes the ocean.
