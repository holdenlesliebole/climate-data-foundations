SECTION ONE

The ideas, slowly

No code in this section. Just the handful of words that everything else today depends on.

Start here

Why we can't just report the average.

Say you measure the ocean at the pier for one summer

You take the average. You get a number. Someone asks: "if you had measured a different summer, would you have got the same number?"

Almost certainly not. So which number is the real one?

That question has a whole toolkit behind it

The honest answer is not one number. It's a number plus a range, and a sentence saying what that range assumes.

What you'll be able to say by 4pm

"The average summer surface-minus-bottom difference at Scripps Pier is 1.33 °C, and if I had a different set of summers I would expect to get something between 1.25 and 1.41 °C."

That second half is today.

Five days at the pier

Let's do it with numbers you can hold in your head.

1.2 Monday. The surface was 1.2 °C warmer than the bottom.
0.9 Tuesday.
2.1 Wednesday.
1.4 Thursday.
0.4 Friday.

Those five numbers are the observations

Each one is a real measurement someone took. They're all different from each other, and none of them is wrong.

Add them and divide by five, and you get 1.2

That single number is a statistic. You made it. It wasn't measured. Nobody ever went to the pier and observed 1.2 as a week.

Now the awkward question

If you'd walked out on five different days, would you still have got 1.2?

Obviously not. So how far off might you be? That range is the third thing, and it's what the whole day is about.

The same three words, written down

Observation, statistic, interval.

An observation

One measurement. One row in your file. 1.2 on Monday.

Real, specific, and already in the past.

A statistic

A number you work out from the observations. The average of 1.2.

An average is one. So is a median, a maximum, or a correlation.

An interval

A range saying how much that statistic would move if you'd collected different data. Perhaps 0.8 to 1.6.

It describes your number, not the ocean.

If you take one thing from this section

People mix up the second and the third constantly, including people who have been doing this for years. We're going to keep them apart on purpose, and I'll keep pointing at which one we're talking about.

The words we'll use today

Plain English first.

population
Everything you wish you could measure. Usually impossible to measure all of it.
sample
The part you actually got.
observation
One measurement. One row.
mean
The average. Add them up, divide by how many.
median
The middle one, when you line them up in order.
standard deviation
How far a typical single measurement sits from the average.
standard error
How much the average itself would wobble if you collected data again.
bootstrap
A way of finding that wobble by reusing the data you already have.

Two of those sound identical and are not

Standard deviation and standard error. We're going to spend a whole slide on the difference, with a picture, because getting them mixed up is the single most common mistake in this material.

Population and sample

You never have all of it.

An everyday version

You want to know the average height of everyone at Scripps. You can't measure everyone, so you measure the forty people in this room.

Everyone at Scripps is the population. The forty of you are the sample. Each person measured is one observation.

Our version today

We want the typical June–August surface-minus-bottom temperature difference at the pier, across all summers, including ones nobody measured.

What we've is the summers in the archive. That's our sample.

The catch, and it's a big one

The forty people in this room are not a random selection of Scripps. Neither are the summers in an archive a random selection of summers.

Rows in a file are not automatically a fair sample. How, when and why the measurements were taken stays part of your analysis. No formula today fixes a problem there.

Saying the symbols

μ is "mew", a Greek letter. It stands for the number we would get if we could measure everything. We never see it.

x̄ is "x-bar". It's the number we actually calculated. It's our best guess at μ, and it isn't μ.

The average, built one piece at a time

You already know how to do this.

x One measurement. A single day's temperature difference.
xi The little i is just a label so we can talk about "the first one", "the second one". x₁, x₂, x₃, and so on.
n How many you've. For us, 8,771.
Σ A capital Greek S. It's shorthand for "add up everything that comes after me". Nothing more than that.
Σ xi So this pair says: add up all the measurements. Nothing more than that.
x̄ Said "x-bar". The bar on top is the convention for "the average of".
x̄ = (1 / n) Σ xi the sample mean

Read it back in words

"Add up all the measurements, then divide by how many there were."

You've done that since primary school. The symbols are shorthand so it fits on one line, and so it means the same thing to a statistician in Osaka.

Also worth having: the median

Line every value up from smallest to largest and take the one in the middle.

The mean gets dragged by a single huge value. The median barely notices it. When those two numbers sit far apart, something is pulling on one side, and you want to know that.

The spread, built one piece at a time

How far is a typical day from the average?

xi − x̄ Take one measurement and subtract the average. Now you know how far off that one day was. Could be positive or negative.
( … )² Square it. Two reasons: minus signs disappear, so 2 below counts the same as 2 above, and big misses count for more than small ones.
Σ Add all those squared distances together, over every day you've.
÷ (n−1) Divide, to get an average squared distance rather than a total that grows with your data.
√ Square root at the end, which undoes the squaring and puts the answer back into °C instead of °C².
s = √[ (1 / (n−1)) Σ (xi − x̄)² ] the standard deviation

Read it back in words

"How far is a typical measurement from the average?"

A small s means everything is bunched up close together. A large s means the values are scattered all over.

Why n−1 rather than n

A small correction. Dividing by n makes the answer come out a bit too small on average, and subtracting one fixes it.

You don't need to prove that. What you do need to know is that the fix assumes your measurements are independent of each other. Ours aren't, and we'll come back to it.

Do this every single time

Check the units.

In °C

The mean. The median. The quantiles. The IQR. The standard deviation. All of these come out in the same units as your data.

In °C²

The variance, because we squared things and didn't square-root them back. This is why we usually report s rather than s².

No units at all

A count. A correlation. A proportion.

Why this is a real check and not busywork

If you calculate something and the units come out impossible, you haven't made a rounding error. You have made a mistake in the calculation or in what you think you're calculating.

It costs five seconds and it catches things nothing else catches.

One tiny example, by hand

Four numbers, all the way through.

1, 2, 3, 4 °Cthe entire dataset

x̄ = 2.5 °C

Σ(xi − x̄)² = 5 °C²

s = √(5 / 3) ≈ 1.29 °C

Follow the arithmetic

Average of 1, 2, 3, 4 is 2.5. The distances from 2.5 are −1.5, −0.5, +0.5, +1.5. Squared: 2.25, 0.25, 0.25, 2.25. That adds to 5.

Divide by n − 1, which is 3, giving 1.67. Square root: about 1.29 °C.

What the two numbers say

The middle of this data is 2.5 °C. A typical value sits about 1.3 °C away from that middle.

What they don't say

Whether the data is lopsided. Whether it has two separate humps. Whether there are gaps. What order it came in.

Two datasets can have the same mean and the same standard deviation and look nothing like each other. So we always plot it too.

Now the real thing

A century of summer days at the pier.

How to read this

Each bar counts how many days had that surface-minus-bottom difference. Tall bar near 0 means lots of days where surface and bottom were about the same.

Two things to notice

It's lopsided. There's a long tail stretching right, so the mean sits above the median. Reporting only "1.32 °C" hides that completely.

And hold on to 1.49 °C, the standard deviation. It's about to be confused with a very different number.

Changing the question

Stop. Different question now.

Everything so far answered this

"How much do individual days vary?"

Answer: about 1.49 °C either side of the average. That's the standard deviation.

Here is a completely different question

"If I went and collected a whole new hundred years of summer days, how different would my average come out?"

That isn't about days. It's about the average itself.

Why they feel like the same question

Both are "how much does it vary". Both come out in °C. Both get called "the spread". But one is about single measurements and one is about a summary of thousands of measurements, and their answers are nowhere near each other.

Next slide has both, drawn on the same axis.

The most important picture today

Same axis. Two different questions.

Top panel: the days

Wide. Individual days really do vary by more than a degree. s = 1.49 °C.

Bottom panel: the average

A spike. Across thousands of imagined repeats, the average barely moves. 0.016 °C.

Both are true at once

Any given day is unpredictable to more than a degree. The long-run average of thousands of days is pinned down very tightly.

That isn't a contradiction. It's the reason averages are useful.

Naming the second one, piece by piece

Standard error.

s The spread of your measurements, from the slide before. For us, 1.49 °C.
n How many measurements. For us, 8,771.
√n The square root of that. √8,771 is about 94.
s / √n Divide one by the other: 1.49 ÷ 94 = 0.016 °C.
SE(x̄) = s / √n the standard error of the mean

Why dividing helps

The bottom of the fraction gets bigger as you collect more data, so the whole thing gets smaller. Gather four times as many days and the number halves.

One condition, and we haven't checked it

This formula only works if your measurements are independent of each other, meaning that knowing one tells you nothing about the next.

Two slides from now we test that on our own data. It turns out to be false, and that changes the answer.

A useful thing to see once

More data buys certainty, not a calmer ocean.

The red line falls

The more days you collect, the more sure you become about the long-run average.

The blue line doesn't move

Individual days are just as variable as they always were. Measuring more of them doesn't calm the ocean down.

The sentence people get wrong

"We have loads of data, so the temperature doesn't vary much." No. Loads of data means you know the average well. Tomorrow is still unpredictable.

The assumption we skipped

Are our days independent?

What this plot is

Every dot is one day. Across the bottom: that day's difference. Up the side: the next day's difference.

What it shows

If days were independent, knowing today would tell you nothing about tomorrow, and this would be a shapeless cloud.

It isn't a cloud. It slopes. r = 0.46. Warm days follow warm days.

So what?

Our 8,771 days are not 8,771 separate pieces of information. Many of them are partly repeats of the day before.

The formula that gave 0.016 °C assumed otherwise. It's arithmetically correct and it's answering a question about data we don't have.

Where dependence comes from

This is normal, not a flaw in your data.

The ocean has memory

A warm patch of water is still there tomorrow. Weather systems last days.

One event, many rows

A single storm can produce a week of unusual readings that all say the same thing.

Seasons

August days resemble other August days more than they resemble January.

Shared equipment

One instrument, one calibration, one processing pipeline behind every row.

What an honest analyst does about it

Not "fix" it. Name it, pick a unit of data that's more defensible, and report how much the answer changes if you had picked differently.

That's the whole of section 3 this afternoon, and it changes our answer by a factor of nearly three.

Ranges · 1 of 2

What a confidence interval actually claims.

x̄ ± t* · SE(x̄)the estimate, plus and minus a multiple of the standard error

The careful sentence

If you repeated the whole sampling procedure many times, and calculated an interval the same way each time, about 95% of those intervals would contain the true value.

The claim is about the method, over many imagined repeats.

An analogy that helps

Think of a ring toss. The method is your throwing arm; the peg is the true value. Saying "95%" describes how often your arm lands a ring over the peg.

Once a specific ring has landed, it either went over the peg or it didn't. The 95% was about your arm, not about that one throw.

Ranges · 2 of 2

The version everyone says, which is wrong.

Try not to say

"There's a 95% probability that the true value is inside this interval."

Once you've calculated one interval, the true value is not moving around inside it. It's fixed. Your interval either covers it or it doesn't.

Does this matter in practice?

Rarely for you, honestly. It matters when a reviewer challenges you, and it matters because the sloppy version leads people to treat the interval as a probability distribution over the truth, which it isn't.

And one more distinction

The sampling distribution is the spread of the statistic across imagined repeats. It isn't the histogram of your data.

That's the two-panel picture from earlier: top was the data, bottom was the sampling distribution.

Bootstrap · the idea

When you would rather not trust a formula.

The problem

The formula s/√n needs independent observations. Ours are not. And for statistics other than the mean, there may be no tidy formula at all.

The trick

We wanted to know what would happen if we collected the data again. We can't. So we pretend the data we've is the world, and draw new samples from it.

Picture it as a hat

Write each of your values on a slip of paper and put them in a hat. Draw one out, write it down, put it back, and draw again, until you've as many as you started with.

Some values get drawn twice, some not at all. Take the average of that new pile. Then do the whole thing a few thousand times.

The spread of those few thousand averages is your answer.

Bootstrap · the steps

Five steps, and one of them is yours alone.

  1. Decide what you're estimating, and what counts as one unit that could be swapped for another.
  2. Draw that many units out of the hat, with replacement.
  3. Calculate your statistic on the new pile.
  4. Do it a few thousand more times.
  5. Take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the results. That's your 95% interval.

Steps 2 to 5 are the computer's job

It does them instantly, thousands of times, without complaining or getting tired.

Step 1 is entirely yours

Is one unit a day? A storm? A month? A whole summer? The computer can't tell you, won't warn you, and the answer changes depending on what you choose.

This afternoon you'll see it change by a factor of 2.6.

Last one before we open a notebook

What none of this repairs.

An unfair sample

A tight interval around unrepresentative data is a very precise description of the wrong thing.

Wrong units or misread flags

A Fahrenheit column or a misunderstood quality flag gives a confident number that's wrong.

Gaps that are not random

If the sensor fails in storms, you're missing exactly the conditions you care about.

Cause

No interval turns "these two move together" into "this one causes that one".

Which is why the order today is what it's

Where the data came from, then look at it properly, then say clearly what you're estimating, and only then put a range on it.

Notebook 07 is the "look at it properly" step, and it comes first for a reason. Do it the other way round and you get a beautifully quantified mistake.

Notebook 07 · section 5 · the trap

A value sits far from the others.

What you'll want to do

Delete it. The plot looks tidier afterwards, the standard deviation drops, and everything feels cleaner.

Why that's dangerous here

Upwelling, storms and marine heatwaves all produce extreme days at this pier that are entirely real. Those days are frequently the scientifically interesting ones.

Deleting values because they're inconvenient is how people accidentally delete their own finding.

The phrase to hold on to

Extreme doesn't automatically mean wrong.

An unusual measurement and a broken measurement look identical in a spreadsheet. Telling them apart takes knowing something about the ocean, not about statistics.

Notebook 07 · section 5 · what to do instead

Impossible, or just unusual?

Impossible → investigate and probably remove

−40 °C at Scripps Pier. A surface reading of 999. A date in the year 1815.

These are instruments failing, or fill values that were never meant to be read as data.

Unusual → keep, and look at it

A 7 °C surface-minus-bottom difference in August. A single very cold week in July.

These are the ocean doing something. Go and find out what.

The defensible procedure

Remove only what you can justify by a physical reason. Write down what you removed and why. If removing it changes your answer, report the answer both ways.

"I dropped three days where the sensor reported −999, which is the archive's missing-data marker" is defensible. "I dropped the outliers" is not.

Run it now, then answer the exit ticket

Run section 5, look at the days around each extreme value, and for each one ask: broken instrument, or real ocean?

Notebook 07 · what you're making

A one-page data health statement.

Six things it contains

Where the data came from and when you got it. The window and quality rule you chose. What is missing, and whether the gaps look random. What you removed and why. One sentence on what this record can support. One sentence on what it can't.

Why this is worth the twenty minutes

Every number you produce for the rest of the week rests on it. Writing it once means you never have to reconstruct from memory what "the good data" meant.

It's also, almost word for word, the paragraph a reviewer will ask you for, and the briefing an AI needs so it stops guessing about your data.

SECTION THREE

Putting a range on it

The data is inspected, so now we can estimate something honestly. The interesting part is not the arithmetic. It's one choice that changes the answer by a factor of nearly three.

Notebook 08 · sections 1 and 2

Calculate the easy answer first, so we can distrust it.

Everyone — open notebook 08 now

  1. Same folder. Open notebooks/08_uncertainty.ipynb, kernel climate-data-foundations.
  2. Run section 1. It rebuilds the checked dataset using yesterday's rules.
  3. Run section 2 and read the two numbers it prints.

What you should see

About 8,771 summer days. A spread of about 1.49 °C. And a standard error of about 0.016 °C.

Taken at face value, that last number says we know the average summer difference to within a few hundredths of a degree.

Believe it for thirty seconds

You have already seen the lag plot. Adjacent days correlate at 0.46. The formula that produced 0.016 assumed they didn't.

So this is not bad arithmetic. It's correct arithmetic answering a question about a dataset we don't have, which is a much harder mistake to spot.

Notebook 08 · section 3

Turn 8,771 days into 100 summers.

Run it now — section 3

This collapses each summer down to a single average, leaving about 100 numbers instead of 8,771.

Why throw away so much

Because those 8,771 rows were never 8,771 independent pieces of information. Days within one summer are heavily related to each other.

Two different summers, though, are much closer to being separate. A hundred summers is a hundred fairly independent observations of "what a summer is like".

What we keep

Each summer's average still contains all of that summer's days. We haven't deleted anything. We have grouped it into units that we can defend treating as exchangeable.

It should feel wasteful

It does, and it's still right. Having 8,771 rows never meant having 8,771 rows' worth of information.

Notebook 08 · section 4

Now do the hat trick, four thousand times.

What this histogram is, and is not

It is not the data. It is not the summers. It is 4,000 answers to the question "what if we had happened to get a different set of summers?"

The width of that pile is the uncertainty.

Reading the interval

Cut off the lowest 2.5% and the highest 2.5% of those 4,000 answers. What is left runs from 1.25 to 1.41 °C.

That's the 95% bootstrap interval, and no formula was involved.

Notebook 08 · section 5 · the point of the whole day

One choice. A 2.6× difference.

Same data. Same statistic. Same code.

The only thing that changed is what counted as one unit in the hat.

Days: the average is pinned to about ±0.03 °C. Summers: about ±0.08 °C.

Which one is right?

The summers, here, because we can see on the lag plot that days are related to each other.

But notice the honest version is not "always pick the wider one". It's that the interval describes your model, not the ocean. You have to be able to defend the model, and you've to report which one you picked.

Notebook 08 · what you're making

Three sentences. Write them before you leave.

1 · The estimate

What you estimated, the value, and the units.

2 · The interval, and the unit you resampled

The range, how you got it, and what counted as one unit. This is the sentence people leave out.

3 · What it doesn't cover

Sampling, instruments, gaps, and the fact that this is one record from one pier.

A worked version, to copy the shape of

"The mean summer surface-minus-bottom difference at Scripps Pier is 1.33 °C, with a 95% bootstrap interval of 1.25 to 1.41 °C, resampling whole summers as the unit. Resampling individual days instead would give an interval 2.6 times narrower, which the day-to-day correlation of 0.46 tells us would be overconfident. This interval says nothing about sensor calibration, about gaps in the record, or about whether these summers represent summers we didn't observe."

Where this leaves you

The number was the easy part.

Look first

A range around unexamined data is precision about the wrong thing.

Name the unit

The computer resamples whatever you tell it to. That choice is the analysis.

Report the model

An interval describes your assumptions as much as it describes the ocean.
